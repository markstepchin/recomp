Why is auto-alignment for progress photos so hard to do well?

Quick answer

Finding a body in a photo is the easy part. Apple's Vision framework finds 19 body points right on an iPhone. The hard part is everything after: picking what to line up against, noticing when the answer is wrong before it fakes progress, and letting you fix it without a fight. The release notes of apps that ship auto-alignment show exactly those problems.

I build a manual-only app, so read this with that in mind

I build Recomp, a progress photo app for iPhone. Recomp aligns by hand with pan, zoom and rotate, and it has no auto-alignment. A post about why auto-alignment is hard helps an app without it, so that's a conflict of interest.

To keep this fair, every claim about another app quotes the developer's own public words or, where marked, App Store reviews, with a link. I checked each one on October 2, 2026. I haven't run a hands-on test yet, so this post has no scores, and where auto-alignment clearly helps, I say so.

Why is a body photo harder to align than a selfie?

Alignment has to hold the camera constant while the body changes. The change is what you want to see: a smaller waist, a wider back. The camera's distance, height and tilt should match between dates. A tool that lines up the wrong thing can hide real change or create fake change.

Over years, almost everything around the body changes too. Rooms, phones, lenses, lighting and mirror distance all drift. Apple's own image-alignment sample says "The best alignment occurs when the content of the input images is very similar." Two check-ins a year apart aren't similar, and that's the whole point of taking them.

Detection is the easy part

Apps use three different techniques under one label. Each anchors on something different, so each should break in a different place. The last column is my expectation, not a measurement.

Technique What it lines up What it ignores Where I expect it to break
Image registration The pixels of the whole frame, mostly background The body A new room, a moved camera
Body-pose landmarks Shoulders and hips found by a model The background Side photos, loose clothing, hidden hips
Face detection The eyes The torso Back photos, a tilted head

Image registration aligns the scene, not you. Apple's Vision framework offers translational registration, which solves a shift only. Its homographic version solves a perspective warp, which can also bend the body. With a tripod in one room, the background stands in for the camera. In a new room, the background no longer tells you anything about the camera.

Body-pose landmarks anchor on joints. A pose model finds points like shoulders and hips, and a fit scales, rotates and shifts each photo to line them up. Apple's model finds up to 19 points, and Google's MediaPipe finds 33. Apple lists its weak spots: people "bent over or upside down" and "obstructive flowing clothing."

Face detection anchors on the eyes. That suits selfie timelapses, and AgeLapse aligns "every photo on your eyes." On a body photo, the head turns on its own, and a back photo has no face.

Apple's tools ship on every recent iPhone, and MediaPipe runs on the phone too. Getting points out of a photo is no longer the barrier.

Why don't progress photo apps get it right?

It has to pick what to line up against. Thumbstone matches "your previous shots," and ProgressCam "registers each new shot against the last." Chaining each photo to the one before it is easy to build. But a small error in every step can add up over years, so photo 200 may drift away from photo 1 even if each pair looks fine. One fixed reference per pose avoids that, but then a new room breaks every photo after it.

It has to know when it's wrong. A wrong alignment looks like a result, not a bug. On a side photo, one hip hides behind the other, so a pose model can grab the wrong one. The photo shifts, and your waist looks different for reasons that have nothing to do with you. Lasmit turned Smart Overlay off by default in version 2026.6 (February 11, 2026). Its note says "when it didn't work, it was causing a lot of confusion."

It has to hand control back without a fight. Once the tool is wrong, you need a fast way to fix one photo. FormLapse 1.3 added "Fine-tune any photo whose auto-alignment was off — just match the skeleton to your body" and a "Review alignment" list. Thumbstone takes the other route and promises "no manual lining up." That only works if the tool is never wrong.

It has to survive updates and big imports. ProgressCam lets you "batch-align in one go" on import. That's where it helps most, and where one quiet error spreads across hundreds of photos. The models underneath change too. GainFrame's 2.32 update (July 22, 2026) opens with "PHOTO ALIGNMENT RESTORED": photos "are once again aligned to your pose reference when saved." For a while, they weren't. Thumbstone's 1.8.2 and 1.8.3 notes both describe better pose detection, so shipping it means maintaining it.

Some apps move the problem to capture. Fitness Camera's Android app watches your pose and fires the shutter "the moment you match your saved pose." That lines up the person before the photo exists instead of fixing pixels after. Its site also says it "aligns your new photo to the previous one automatically," but the Play listing doesn't, so I count that as unconfirmed.

Who handles failure best?

There's almost no public evidence that body auto-alignment holds up. The newer body apps that auto-align have between 0 and 68 US ratings each. Across them, there's a single review that mentions how well it aligns.

Face apps handle failure and fixes best. AgeLapse is open source, and it tells you when it fails. Its docs point you to photos marked "⚠ 'No Faces Found'" or "✗ 'Failed'". Its README says "A saved manual edit takes precedence over the automatic alignment and is re-applied on every subsequent re-stabilization". The tool makes a guess, and your fix always wins.

Reviews name what goes wrong when it doesn't. One user in Lasmit's App Store reviews says its photo feature "zooms in on some pictures and out on others making it hard to actually compare" (4★, July 2025). Another asks for "not such MASSIVE borders around each photo" (3★, January 2025). Add the silent failures behind Lasmit's note, and that's the list. These come from a few vocal reviewers, so they show the kinds of failure, not how often it happens.

Adobe doesn't ship cross-shot alignment on iPhone outside HDR capture. Photoshop on iPhone doesn't mention Auto-Align anywhere in its listing, release notes or launch post. Lightroom aligns only inside its own HDR capture, where three shots are "automatically aligned, merged, deghosted, and tonemapped in the app" (Adobe).

Where auto-alignment does help

For a large import of old photos, a first pass saves real time. Lining up hundreds of photos by hand is slow, and a decent first guess beats a blank start. It also helps face timelapses, where the eyes are a stable anchor. The question isn't whether detection works, but whether you can trust the result without checking every photo.

Why I align by hand in Recomp

I left auto-alignment out of Recomp on purpose. A comparison is only proof if you know why the two photos line up. If an algorithm picks the framing, I have to check every photo it touched before I can trust what I see.

So alignment in Recomp is direct: you pan, pinch and rotate either photo right in Compare, and it saves when you lift your finger. If a comparison looks off, you know why, and the fix is one gesture. More on how that works on the Compare page.

The better fix is a better photo. Coming in Recomp 3.6, the in-app camera shows your last photo of the same pose as a see-through ghost overlay, so you line up before the shot. My routine for consistent photos is in how to take progress photos for a recomp.

What's still unresolved

Nobody publishes how often their auto-alignment is wrong. The apps above describe what their alignment does. None that I found says how often it fails on side or back photos, or how far the chain drifts over years. Those are the numbers that matter, so I'll measure the techniques on my own archive, with my own manual alignment as the answer key, in a follow-up post. If you've measured this on your own photos, I'd like to see it: markstepchin@gmail.com.
