How I review progress photo apps

By Mark Stepchin · Updated [CONFIRM: publish date]

Quick Answer

I download every app I review, pay for the subscription if there is one, and test it with real photos and weights from my own fitness transformation. I measure how many taps and how much time it takes to add a check-in, compare dates, and align photos. The app I'm looking for lets me finish a check-in in under 30 seconds, keeps the photos front and center, and never sends my photos to a third-party server.

Why are my reviews different?

Most of the reviews I read when I was looking for an app for myself hadn't downloaded and tested the app. They relied mostly on the App Store listing for their information.

For my reviews, I download the app, set up an account, and pay for the subscription if one exists. Then I use real photos and weights from my fitness transformation.

Full disclosure: I build Recomp, a free progress photo app, and I hold it to the same bar as every other app. I don't run ads, and I don't use affiliate links.

Who am I?

I've taken progress photos every week since June 6, 2023. During this time, I bulked from 165 to 195 lb from June 6, 2023 to May 21, 2024, then cut to 155 lb from May 21, 2024 to August 18, 2025.

Photo comparison kept me confident in my plan and my progress even when I couldn't see the progress in the mirror. Tracking my progress with photos was an essential part of my strategy and my feedback loop.

I already knew what I did and didn't need, because I'd used my trainer's app. Today I have a Photos collection with 450+ progress photos from my years of tracking, and it's the library I test every app with.

Why friction kills consistency

Photos only work as a feedback loop if you take them every week. If a check-in takes more than a minute and a few taps, consistency drops.

Testing progress photo apps is mentally draining. When I was looking for an app for myself, I only got through 3 or 4 apps in the first sitting before I was completely frustrated and drained. Every new app is a new system to learn, and most apps weren't intuitive to use.

You download each app with high hopes, and most of those hopes end in disappointment. That's why I measure every app in taps and seconds.

What does a progress photo app need?

Four baseline features: it stores front, side and back photos and weight, has a compare screen, aligns photos for an accurate comparison, and sends notifications for consistency. Having a feature isn't enough, so I also judge the quality of each one. These are my 6 principles, with what I measure under each.

1. A check-in should be effortless

A check-in should take minimal taps. Weight should come from a smart scale through Apple Health when that's available. When it isn't, a weight slider is 1 tap plus a drag, which beats typing the weight in. I should be able to select all 3 photos in the same photo picker instead of opening the picker 3 times.

What I measure: how many taps and how much time it takes to add a new check-in. Great is under 30 seconds.

2. Alignment should be effortless

Different people prefer different approaches to alignment, so the more an app offers, the better. Auto align is never ideal on any app, but it gets you 75% of the way there. Other approaches are a ghost overlay, several gridline variations for different photo-taking styles, and comparing photos side by side in the compare view.

What I measure: ease of alignment, gridline quality, whether there's a ghost overlay and auto align, and whether I can align in the side by side view.

3. Comparison should be effortless

I judge how cumbersome the navigation gets with dozens or hundreds of photos. I also check how easy it is to align photos during a compare session.

What I measure: how easy it is to compare, and how long and how many taps it takes to compare 9 different dates. Great is effortless, even with dozens or hundreds of photos.

4. The photos are the star

A progress photo app is a photo album app. Photos should be front and center and take up most of the screen. Every other button should sit where it doesn't distract from the main job of the app, which is to display and compare the photos.

What I measure: how much of the screen the photos take, and whether the buttons get in their way.

5. Data privacy

Are your photos ever sent to a third-party server? That's the question I answer for every app, and the answer I want is no.

6. Quality of life

Quality of life covers data import and export, screen privacy, Apple Health integration, and more. I only weigh it once the basics are right. The full list of questions is in the quality of life section below, including notification flexibility, since notifications for consistency are a baseline feature.

What does a great check-in look like?

My check-in takes under 30 seconds. First thing in the morning, I snap my photos with my camera app and weigh myself. As I'm walking over to the coffee machine, I open the notification for my check-in, add the 3 photos, and update the date if it changed.

Then I hit save and compare my results to last week, 2 weeks ago, and a month ago. The whole thing takes a minute or two, including the comparison. Every app I test gets measured against that routine.

Quality of life, once the basics work

After the baseline features, these are the questions I check.

  1. Bulk import: Can I import my past photos in bulk, does it read the dates from the photo metadata, and how does it handle hundreds of photos?
  2. Data import and export: Can I import my weight from a CSV, get my photos back out, and leave without being locked in?
  3. Apple Health: Does it sync my weight from Apple Health?
  4. Screen privacy: Can I lock the app with Face ID or an app lock, and does it hide my photos in the app switcher?
  5. Backup and a new phone: Are my photos backed up, for example to iCloud, and can I move everything to a new phone?
  6. Reminders: Can I set the day, the time and how often I get reminded?
  7. In-app camera: Does it have a camera with a ghost overlay and pose guides, such as upper body, full body and a 3x3 grid?
  8. Units: Does it support both metric and imperial?
  9. Body measurements: Can I add body measurements if I want them, without being forced to?
  10. Sharing: Can I make a shareable before/after image?
  11. Timelapse: Can it make a timelapse of my photos?
  12. Account and originals: Does it work without an account, and does it leave my originals untouched in Photos?
  13. Editing: Can I edit a check-in's date, and save a partial check-in when I skip a pose?
  14. Polish: Does it have dark mode, and is it free of ads?

How do I test each app?

I run every app through the same 7 steps, using photos from my 450+ photo collection.

  1. Download the app, go through onboarding, and pay for the subscription.
  2. Add 6 check-ins, each with a weight and front, side and back photos.
  3. Check whether the app reads the date from the photos. If it doesn't, enter each date manually.
  4. Check whether the app imports health data. If it doesn't, enter the weight manually.
  5. Test the alignment and comparison features.
  6. Test the quality of life features.
  7. Test bulk import if it's available: how long the import takes, and how the app performs with hundreds of photos.

How do I judge each app?

I don't use a score. For each app, I call out what's good and what's bad. Then I give a verdict in plain words on whether I recommend it, and say who I'd recommend it for.

Which apps have I reviewed this way?

So far I've tested 12 apps. Nine are dedicated progress photo apps: GainFrame, Fit-Stitch, Lasmit Progress, Metamorph, Progress Pics Photo Then & Now, Progress Pics: Body Tracker (Thumbstone), Body Tracker: Photo & Measure (Igor Korolev), Photo Compare Before After, and Progress: Photo Journal. The other three are fitness apps with a photo feature: Hevy, MacroFactor and MyFitnessPal.
